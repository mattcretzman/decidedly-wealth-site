Why Your Family Business Team Keeps Clashing

By Sanger Smith · September 15, 2026

You hired smart people. You gave them clear roles. So why does it feel like nobody is on the same page? The answer probably has less to do with your team and more to do with you.

I was out to dinner with a friend of mine, Steve. The waiter was slow, forgetting our order, the whole deal. I thought I was keeping my frustration to myself. I was not. Steve finally looked at me and said, "Hey, man. You're kind of ruining the night for everyone else. Just chill out."

That one hit me right in the chest. I thought we were all commiserating because this person was bad at their job. But no. Everybody else at the table was perfectly capable of having a good night regardless. I was the only one who couldn't let it go.

That moment stuck with me because it captures something I see in business owners all the time. We assume everyone sees the world the way we do. We assume our priorities are obvious, our frustrations are shared, and our way of operating is the right way. And when people don't match that, we label them. They're slow. They don't get it. They don't care.

But here's the thing. We are all the idiot in somebody else's story.

Self-awareness is the starting line. I can't be a good leader if I don't know what my weaknesses are or how I'm going to show up on my worst day. Someone told me a long time ago that we judge other people by their actions and we judge ourselves by our intentions. My report's not on time because I was trying to make it perfect. His report's not on time because he doesn't care. Same outcome. Completely different story we tell ourselves.

My dad and I both lean toward what behavioral experts call "red" personality traits. Competitive, impatient, task-driven. Anything short of perfection and we're thinking, "Come on, man. Make a decision." That works in some rooms. It clears other rooms out entirely.

I've got a friend, Dererick Kenny, who approaches things completely differently than I do. He's very people-oriented. He's always got a circle of people around him. He's never by himself. Meanwhile, look at what I'm wearing. Khakis and a solid blue shirt. I'm trying to blend in. We're just wired differently at a fundamental level.

The real problem isn't personality differences. It's motivation. When I sit down with business owners, I see them make the same mistake over and over. They assume the only way to reward their team is to pay them more. The owner is motivated by money, so he figures everybody else is too. He ends up giving them something they weren't even looking for, costing himself something that mattered a lot to him, and getting zero extra motivation out of it. That employee just keeps doing the same job. Now they're getting paid more to do it. What they actually wanted was more flexibility, more purpose in their work, more recognition, more autonomy within their role.

I was at a conference in Colorado Springs talking to a guy who worked as an employee at another firm. He starts asking me all these questions about going independent. I'm giving him advice based on what motivated me, things like autonomy and the ability to make your own calls. He kept pushing back. Finally I asked, "Why do you want to be independent?" He said, "So I can make more money."

I told him straight. Embarking upon the journey of entrepreneurship purely for the money? You ought to stay an employee, man. That's not what drives you through the sleepless nights and the risk. But here's the key. I can't judge him for that. His motivation comes from his upbringing, his family, his circumstances. It's different from mine. That doesn't make it wrong.

You don't need everyone to be like you. You need everyone pulling in the same direction. My dad asked our guest, Thomas Erikson, whether it's better to surround yourself with people like you to reduce conflict, or to bring in people who are different. Thomas's answer was clear. Diversity is great. Different perspectives give you broader problem-solving. Extroverts and introverts, task-oriented people and people-oriented people, they all bring something you don't have.

But you need a leader who can work with all of those differences. If you're tilted to one side, you'll understand the people who are like you and completely miss the ones on the other side of the fence.

I've found this in my own work. There are advisors I will pair with to work with a client because they approach the relationship very differently than I do. They're asking questions about family that I might forget to ask because I've been focused on other objectives. That pairing produces a better client relationship than either of us would create alone. Same thing in a marriage, raising kids. You get better results when the people around you complement your strengths instead of duplicating them.

The conversation most family businesses never have. Here's where it gets really challenging. In a family business, everyone thinks the important stuff has already been said. You had a conversation about values and mission at some point. Maybe you even wrote a mission statement. Then you set it on the table and didn't talk about it with your team for six months.

That's not good enough. Every time you bring someone new on board, they're bringing different values into the mix. Different reasons for being there. You need to be having that conversation regularly, not once a year, not in an email. Thomas put it bluntly. If you don't bring up that sense of purpose, that mission, around the dinner table weekly, you're going to lose it.

It doesn't have to be world-conquering. It can be something small. But you have to keep it alive.

And it goes deeper than just agreeing on the mission. Even when everyone in a family agrees on something like "carry on the legacy," every person in that family has their own interpretation of what that means. For one person, it means preserving grandma's original recipe. For another, it just means keeping the company alive and providing a way to earn a living. Same words. Completely different meanings. You have to dig past the surface and ask, "What do we actually mean by what we're saying?"

Competing with yourself instead of everyone else. Business owners, especially the competitive ones, tend to measure themselves against the competition. We want to beat the Andersons across the street. We want to be number one. But Thomas made a point that stuck with me. There's always going to be somebody out there who can beat you. If you compete with yourself instead, raise your own number by five percent next year, you're actually developing. You can channel that competitive drive in a way that doesn't eat you alive.

And here's the other side of it. If you don't have any competitive drive at all, you tend not to celebrate the wins. Even when you do something fantastic, it's just another day at the office. That's a problem too. We need those moments where we pop the champagne and say, "We did it." Because if you never celebrate, your team stops caring whether they win or not.

Financial goals aren't really financial goals. A lot of our competitors focus on helping clients "meet their financial goals." In my experience, I don't meet a lot of people who actually have financial goals. When they do, those goals are always connected to something bigger. Nobody wants to retire at 62 just to beat their friend who retired at 63. They want to retire at 62 so they can spend more time volunteering at their church. Nobody wants to buy a vacation home just to look successful. They want to buy it because having memories outside their community with their kids and grandchildren matters to them.

The number is just a measurement. The motivation underneath it is what actually moves people.

Where to start. Thomas left us with something practical. Know where you're weak when it comes to making decisions. If you're too impatient, you're making calls without enough information. Work on your patience. If you're too spontaneous, jumping at every shiny new idea, you need to slow down and look at the data. If you're the kind of person who avoids decisions entirely, sleeping on it and then sleeping on it some more, make any decision. Break it into small pieces and start somewhere. And if you're the analytical type who needs one more book, one more seminar, one more podcast before you act, stop preparing and do something.

Self-awareness isn't just about knowing your personality type. It's about understanding how you show up, what drives the people around you, and whether you're building a team or just collecting a group of individuals who happen to work in the same building.

What Healthy Financial Control Actually Looks Like

By RJ Finley · September 15, 2026

There is a moment most business owners hit where they realize the thing they have been chasing, total control, is the exact thing holding them back.

We had Derrick Kinney on the Decidedly Podcast recently. Derrick is a former wealth advisor who built a top-ranked financial planning practice, sold it, and is now coaching entrepreneurs and business owners through the media and his own podcast. He has been on CNBC, Fox Business, CNN. He has had Matthew McConaughey and Mel Robbins on his show. The guy has range.

But what made the conversation stick was something he said about control. Not in business operations. In life. In family. In the decisions that actually shape what your wealth is for.

Derrick told us about his youngest son Dylan. They had a contentious relationship. Derrick was constantly on him about his messy room, his bathroom, the usual stuff dads zero in on. Then, as Dylan started his senior year of high school, Derrick made a decision that changed everything.

He stopped trying to control his son's behavior and started treating him like a man.

Extended the curfew. Stopped nagging about the room. Had real, candid conversations about life instead. Dylan fumbled a couple times. But the relationship transformed. And Derrick summed it up in a way I think every business owner needs to hear: "I am simply a man of influence. I am not a man of control in my kids' life."

That hit me. Because the same pattern shows up in business every single day.

The founder who cannot leave. Derrick used to take four weeks off every year, eventually stretching it to two full months. Not because he was lazy. Because it forced him to build systems, delegate authority, and create an organization that could function without him standing in the middle of it.

He referenced the movie The Founder about Ray Kroc and McDonald's. That scene where they sketch out every move on a tennis court in chalk, erase it, redo it, over and over until there are no wasted steps. That is the kind of operational clarity most business owners never build because they refuse to step away long enough to see what is missing.

And here is the harder truth. When Derrick finally sold his practice in 2019, one of the hardest parts was calling each client individually to tell them. That took a month and a half, about 15 to 20 calls a day. He broke down in tears after the last one. Every call was like pressing play on a video of their history together.

He told us he kept notes from those conversations. Not for nostalgia. Because he knew there would be dark days ahead where he would question the decision. And when those days came, he would go back to those notes and be reminded that he made the right call.

One client told him, "Derek, if you are going to have a midlife crisis, just go buy a Corvette. It is a whole lot cheaper than selling your business."

That is funny. But it also tells you something. People on the outside have a hard time understanding why someone would walk away from something that is working. Derrick called it entering "the Misunderstood Land." People understand someone with a job and a business card. But when you sell and start reinventing yourself, the world does not know what box to put you in.

He was not retiring. He was rewiring. But everywhere he went, people assumed he was done. And at first, it really bothered him.

So why did he sell? During a sabbatical in July of 2019, sitting in the Boston W Hotel overlooking the harbor, he asked himself three questions: How can I be a better husband? How can I be a better father? How can I be a better business owner?

Then he made a list of everything he wanted to do next. Write a book. Launch a podcast. Coach. Speak. Consult. He called it "Derek 2.0."

Being a financial advisor was not on the list.

It did not just slip off. It did not make the cut. Twenty-five years of building a top-ranked practice, and the thing he had given his career to simply was not part of the next version of himself. He compared it to listing the five most important people in your life and realizing your wife was not on it.

Two hours into his sabbatical, he called his wife. She had been thinking the same thing. Totally supportive. And just like that, the decision was made.

But selling was not about quitting. It was about handing people up. Derrick told us he did not want to just hand off his clients. He wanted to hand them up. His measure of success was simple. If his clients were having coffee with him a year after the sale, he wanted them to walk up and thank him for finding someone better to carry the relationship forward.

That is a financial advisor who understands what the job is actually about.

The family goal-setting exercise. For the past ten years, Derrick has walked his family through a goal-setting exercise right before New Year's. All four kids and his wife. About two hours. They go through a couple categories and talk about where they want to grow.

The first couple years, the kids were furious. Practically throwing things. "I cannot believe you are wasting my time." Now, ten years later, they bring their friends to it. His oldest daughter told a couple at lunch recently that family goal-setting was one of the biggest influences on her life. Specifically, that her parents would ask, "Based on our goals, what can we do to support you with yours?"

That is influence. Not control. And it took years to build.

The investment story. During COVID, when everyone was back around the dinner table, Derrick's family would play a simple game at dinner. Highs and lows. What was your high of the day? What was your low? Most of his answers had been pretty boring. Then one day he told the kids he had lost a couple thousand dollars on a bad investment.

You could have heard a pin drop. His kids were riveted. Dad messed up. They had been waiting for this moment their entire lives.

And something shifted. The more Derrick shared his failures, the more his kids trusted him. He realized he had been wasting time trying to look like the perfect parent when being imperfect was the thing that actually built the connection. His line was simple: "The way to be the perfect parent is to be more imperfect."

I will add this. Sanger made a great point during the episode about watching his dad share failures. When Sean told Sanger about things that did not work in business, Sanger did not have to learn those lessons the hard way. The opportunities would come up years later, and Sanger could skip right past them. That is a real competitive advantage. Not in finance. In life.

The money belief that holds people back. Derrick is direct about this. There is a cultural narrative that says money is bad, or that wealthy people must have gotten there through something underhanded. His response is practical. If you are frustrated with how much Amazon's CEO makes, go buy Amazon stock. Make money while you are complaining.

His philosophy is straightforward. Make more money so you can do more good with it. If there is a giving need at your church or community, do not just scrape together a donation. Go make more money by adding more value to people, and then fund the goal. That reframe turns wealth into fuel instead of something to feel guilty about.

The simplest business advice in the episode. One of Derrick's coaching clients kept telling him about a project that was not getting done. They had talked about it three times. The client said he just had not made time to work through the problem with his admin. Derrick's advice was absurdly simple. "Have her put it on the calendar. Why are you carrying 100% of this yourself?"

The light bulb went off. This was someone early in his business, maybe his first or second employee. And the most transformative thing anyone had said to him was: you do not have to do everything.

That is what healthy control looks like. Not gripping tighter. Deciding what to let go of.

What I took from the conversation. Derrick's final tip was borrowed from the movie 42, the Jackie Robinson story. Before Jackie steps off the bus to face the press, his handler tells him, "Just like you see the ball come in slow at the plate, see these questions come in slow too."

Sanger's take was that when you are focused on the highest aim, the day-to-day stops being overwhelming. You are not thinking about where your hands are on the bat. You are focused on putting the bat on the ball. And when you do that, everything slows down.

That is the difference between control and influence. Control is gripping every detail so tight nothing moves without you. Influence is knowing what matters, building the systems, and trusting the people around you to carry it forward.

If you are a business owner who cannot step away for a week without everything falling apart, that is not strength. That is a warning sign. And it is the kind of thing a good financial advisor should be willing to say out loud.

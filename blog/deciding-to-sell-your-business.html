Deciding to Sell Your Business Without Losing Everything

By Sanger Smith · September 15, 2026

It took me about five years of planning to sell my business. Ownership structures, stock accumulation plans, buyout plans, job descriptions. All the things I felt I needed in place to do an effective sale. Most owners never get that far.

There was this bakery sandwich place near us in University Plaza. My favorite spot in town. Little local place, wonderful location, super convenient, always packed. Best cookies and baked goods around. You could go anytime. Grab a soup, snag a cookie on the way out. Universally beloved. Nobody had ever had a bad experience there.

Then one day the owner posts on Facebook. Rents are rising, tough decision, we're closing. Might be back one day.

We knew that was never going to happen. That place is gone forever.

And the thing that got me was this: if she wanted out, why not sell? I would have bought that business. It was packed every single day. She could have sold the bakery, the recipes, the name, the location. Something. But she just shut the doors. Two years later, it's still empty. And it always will be. You can't take a two-year break from business and come back. Everybody's gone.

I think that plagues so many small business owners. She probably saw it as just quitting her job. Didn't recognize the value in that name and that location. Could have sold it to anybody and gotten something.

The thing I always think about when I'm meeting with a business owner is this. You are either going to die in place working, or you are going to sell your business. You are not going to retire from it. You have to sell it. And in many cases, that business is your largest single asset.

I had somebody come in years ago who came to us late. He said, "I sold my business. Need to talk to you." And I'm thinking, I wish we had this conversation a few years ago. We could have done it the right way from a tax standpoint, from a business continuation standpoint. We could have increased the value. Maybe transferred shares to a charity, deducted that, pulled money out. There are ways to tie revenue to bonus targets. All kinds of things we could have done.

But he came in with a big fat check. So we started mapping out how he was going to live on it. Plenty of money. Until he started spending. He didn't tell me he wanted to buy a farm. So he buys the farm, needs a bunch of money for land, and we had already whittled things down for tax purposes he hadn't considered. Then the farm shrank it further. Then $200,000 for irrigation. Before you know it, everything has to go right for it to work. Then the market dropped, he panicked, cashed out at the bottom. All within about 12 months. Heartbreaking.

So how do you actually do this well? On the podcast, I sat down with Ray Sclafani, the founder and CEO of ClientWise. He spent 20 years at Alliance Bernstein, coaches business owners on building enduring firms, and he sold an equity stake in his own company after years of deliberate planning. His take on exit planning starts with three questions.

First: what do you want for an exit value? Not a vague number. A real one. I think of that as the wealth gap. What do you need to be able to do what you want after the sale, and what do you have now? Maybe you sell today and the business is worth $10 million. But you need $15 million. How do we close that gap? That changes your timeline, your strategy, everything.

Second: how do you want your clients and customers cared for when you are no longer there? This one hits differently when you sit with it. You have built relationships over decades. People trust you. They share things with you they do not share with anyone else. When you sell, that trust has to transfer. And Ray put it well. The biggest fear a business owner has when they sell is that their clients will be upset. The second biggest fear? That the clients will not be upset at all. Because it is an emotional separation, not just a financial transaction.

Third: what is the time to exit? Not retirement. Ray does not like that word. Retire means to put down. It is more about a transition into the next season of your life. Most owners have an idea in their head about when they might want to slow down. But it does not have to be all or nothing. Look at my dad. He sold the business but stayed on in a consulting capacity, still helping the firm succeed in a new way. He is not a controlling owner anymore, but he did not just vanish.

Once you are clear on those three things, then you can start building the real plan. Maybe you need to develop your team. Maybe the next generation of leaders needs to grow into bigger roles. Maybe you need to get your benchmarks in order, clean up your P&L, understand your operating expenses. Ray's firm runs clients through about 120 questions and produces a 50-page report ranking the business across seven key areas. That is where the gap analysis happens. That is where you see what is actually ready and what is not.

The baton handoff is the part most people get wrong. My dad ran track in high school, and he uses this analogy all the time. When you hand off a baton in a relay race, there is a mark on the track. When that runner crosses it, the next person takes off. Then you get close, you say the word, they reach back and grab it. There is a split second where you are both holding it, both running together. And then you let go.

But that is not where it ends. You keep running. You are encouraging them, cheering them on. You do not just hand it off and turn away. And if you try to stop on a dime right after the handoff, you are going to tear your ACL. You have to keep jogging for a little bit. That is the personal plan. That is the part after the sale.

Because here is what we know. People who sell their businesses or retire without a "what's next" are sicker. They do not live as long. They are less happy. Everything is worse. Because they do not have a purpose anymore. For 20 or 30 years, they knew exactly who they were the moment their feet hit the floor. Then one day, that is just gone.

Ray's coaching program asks business owners to start imagining what life looks like beyond the workplace. What is your next ambition? Where is the next impact you want to have? And at the same time, they ask the next generation to imagine what they would do differently if they were running the firm today. When both sides work on that concurrently, the handoff gets a lot smoother.

One thing Ray said that stuck with me is a quote from David Foster. "Everything I've ever let go of has claw marks on it." Owners who hold on too tightly to their businesses have claw marks all over them. And the next generation sees it. They say things like, "He won't let go of those clients. He won't let go of those relationships." That is not a transition. That is a stall.

My biggest takeaway from our conversation with Ray was this. Owning a business is like dating. You are either going to break up or get married. When you own a business, you are either going to die or sell. Most of us do not want to die owning our businesses. But most of us do not have succession plans. Reframing it that way is motivating to get a plan in place.

Five years of planning is not too much. Starting today is not too early. The owners who do this well are the ones who start before they are ready to leave.

Your Portfolio Doesn’t Care Who Won

By RJ Finley · November 4, 2026

If you have ever caught yourself believing the economy would be in real trouble if the wrong candidate won, you are in very large company, and I would like you to look at what has actually happened before you let that belief make a financial decision for you.

The fear is easy to understand. Campaigns tend to describe the other side as a threat to your retirement and your business, and when that message arrives every day for months it starts to feel less like advertising and more like a forecast. The moves that follow from it sound responsible, like holding off on investing the cash you have been saving, leaving the portfolio alone until you see how things shake out, or pushing back a decision about your company until you know who will be in the White House.

What the numbers show

Capital Group looked at the S&P 500 from 1933 through 2023 and sorted the years by who held power in Washington. In the years when one party controlled the White House, the House, and the Senate, the index averaged 14.4% a year. In the years when one party held both chambers of Congress and the president belonged to the other party, it averaged 11.7%, and in the years when Congress itself was split, it averaged 13.7%. The gap between the best of those three and the worst is 2.7 percentage points, which is a fairly narrow band for a stretch of history that includes the 1930s, World War II, the inflation of the 1970s, and the financial crisis of 2008.

Capital Group sums it up this way: “History shows that stocks have done well regardless of the political makeup of Washington.” These are averages across nearly a century, and past results are not a forecast, so I would never ask you to bet that any single year will look like them. What I would ask is that you notice there is no combination of power in that data where the market stopped working.

Why the fear is louder than the result

Markets can certainly feel jumpy around an election, and I would never tell you the weeks before and after a vote are calm. But a portfolio owns companies, and companies earn money when customers keep buying, employees keep showing up, and products keep shipping, which happens under administrations of both parties. Policy can help or hurt a particular industry, and if you own a business in one of them you may feel that more directly than most, yet in my view a diversified portfolio responds far more to the overall economy than to which party gains a few seats.

What waiting costs you

When you decide to wait until the outcome is clear, you are quietly betting that the next stretch will be worse than the average one, and the record above gives you very little support for that bet. In the meantime the cash you set aside for your plan stays set aside, the business decision stays on your desk, and in my experience the moment it finally feels safe to move is rarely the moment that serves you best, because there is always another election, another Fed meeting, or another headline waiting behind the one you were using as your signal.

Decide what changes your plan

A plan should change, but the reasons it changes should come from your life and not from the news.

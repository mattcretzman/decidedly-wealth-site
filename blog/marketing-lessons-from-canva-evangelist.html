Marketing lessons from Canva’s chief evangelist.

By Sanger D. Smith, CEPA™ September 2026 7 min read

Guy Kawasaki was Apple’s original evangelist. He helped launch the Macintosh. He’s now the chief evangelist at Canva. And when he sat down with us on The Decidedly Podcast, the first thing he told us was a story about a company trying to screw him over.

That’s Guy. No pretense, no corporate polish — just straight talk about what works in business and what doesn’t. And for family business owners in Fort Worth who are trying to grow, differentiate, and eventually sell, the lessons he shared are worth more than most marketing courses.

The opposite test.

This was the single biggest takeaway from our conversation, and it’s deceptively simple: if you’re not saying the opposite of your competitors, you’re not saying anything different.

Think about that. How many businesses say “We provide excellent customer service”? All of them. Nobody says “We provide mediocre customer service.” So it’s not a differentiator. It’s wallpaper.

The opposite test works like this: take your marketing message and flip it. If the opposite sounds ridiculous — if no competitor would ever say it — then your message isn’t actually saying anything. You need to find the thing that your competitor would disagree with.

For example: “We start with your life, not your portfolio.” The opposite? “We start with your portfolio, not your life.” Most wealth managers actually DO start with the portfolio — so our statement passes the opposite test. It’s a real differentiator, not just noise.

A lot of it is luck.

Guy said something that most business gurus won’t: a lot of success is luck. He was honest about it. Venture capitalists will tell you they’re great judges of leadership, technology, and markets. Then he pointed out that the same firms that backed Apple and Google also backed spectacular failures.

His point wasn’t that skill doesn’t matter. It’s that luck matters more than most people admit. And understanding that changes how you make decisions:

  • You take more shots because any one of them could be the lucky one
  • You don’t beat yourself up over failures that were partially random
  • You stay humble about successes that were partially random too
  • You build systems instead of relying on one big bet

For a business owner thinking about growth, this is liberating. You don’t need the perfect marketing strategy. You need to be in the game, testing, iterating, and staying visible long enough for luck to find you.

Evangelism vs. marketing.

Guy draws a sharp line between marketing and evangelism. Marketing is about transactions — getting someone to buy. Evangelism is about belief — getting someone to care.

Apple didn’t market the Macintosh. They evangelized it. They convinced people that personal computing was going to change the world. The product sold itself because people believed in what it represented.

For family business owners, the lesson is: stop selling features and start sharing what you believe in. Your customers, your employees, and your eventual buyers all respond to conviction. A business built on belief is worth more than a business built on transactions.

What this means for your business.

Whether you’re trying to grow your business or prepare it for a sale, Guy’s principles apply directly:

  1. Run the opposite test on your marketing. If your competitor could say the same thing, it’s not working.
  2. Acknowledge the role of luck and build systems that maximize your surface area for it.
  3. Evangelize, don’t just market. What does your company believe in? Lead with that.
  4. Engage with new technology. Guy specifically called out AI and new platforms — the business owners who lean in will have an advantage over those who wait.

And if you’re building a business you eventually want to sell, a strong brand built on differentiation and belief is worth dramatically more than a commodity business competing on price. That’s not just marketing advice — it’s exit planning advice.

Post-Exit Wealth

Financial freedom after selling your business.

By Sanger D. Smith, CEPA™ September 2026 7 min read

Here's a stat that'll mess with your head: retirees are, on average, happier than the general population. But there are also more retirees who are depressed than in the general population.

Both things are true at the same time. And if you're a business owner thinking about selling and stepping back, that should get your attention.

Jamie Hopkins explained this on The Decidedly Podcast. Jamie is one of the sharpest minds in retirement planning — and what he shared about the behavioral and emotional side of retirement is something every business owner needs to hear before they sell.

The retirement happiness paradox.

Here's what's happening: when people retire, they don't cluster in the middle. They move to the extremes. A lot of people shift upward on the happiness spectrum — they're relieved, free, energized by the possibilities. The average goes up.

But a significant number shift downward. They lose purpose, routine, identity, social connection. They thought retirement would feel like freedom. Instead it feels like a void.

You don't get a lot of people sitting in the middle. You get more happy people and more depressed people. Which side you land on depends almost entirely on one thing: whether you planned for the emotional transition, not just the financial one.

Money solves the money problem.

I've worked with business owners who sold for more money than they could ever spend. And some of them are miserable six months later.

It sounds crazy, right? You just got the biggest payday of your life. How can you be unhappy?

Because money solves the money problem. It doesn't solve the identity problem, the purpose problem, or the "what do I do on a Tuesday morning" problem. If you've spent 30 years getting up at 6 AM to run a business, and suddenly that's gone, the silence can be deafening.

The rules change.

Jamie made a great point about how household rules shift after major life transitions. When you're running a business, you and your spouse might have unspoken agreements about who handles what. Maybe you do all the cooking because you like it. Maybe your spouse manages the household because you're never home.

Then you sell the business, and suddenly you're home all the time. Those old rules don't apply anymore. And if you don't have a conversation about the new rules, resentment builds fast.

This is the stuff nobody talks about in exit planning. It's not in the spreadsheets. But it makes or breaks the transition.

Aligning actions with beliefs.

My biggest takeaway from Jamie's episode was about alignment. He talked about how we all say certain things are important to us — family, health, faith, community — but we don't always act as if we believe it.

If our actions don't match our stated beliefs, that belief isn't as strong as we think it is.

Jamie made a career move specifically to be closer to his family. That's alignment. He didn't just say family was important — he restructured his life around it.

For business owners approaching an exit, this is the key question: are your actions aligned with what you say matters? If you say family is the priority but you're working 70 hours a week with no exit plan, there's a gap. And that gap will only get wider.

How to land on the right side.

You want to be in the "happier" group, not the "more depressed" group. Here's what the research and my experience both point to:

  • Plan for the life, not just the money. Financial planning is necessary but not sufficient. What will you do? Who will you spend time with? What gives you purpose?
  • Start before you sell. Don't wait until the wire hits your account to figure out your post-exit identity. Start building it while you still have the structure of the business to lean on.
  • Have the household conversation. Talk to your spouse about how daily life will change. New routines, new dynamics, new expectations. Get ahead of it.
  • Stay connected. The biggest risk in retirement is isolation. Board seats, mentoring, community involvement, coaching — build social structures that replace the ones the business provided.
  • Give yourself permission to grieve. Selling a business is a loss, even when it's a gain. You're allowed to feel that. Acknowledging it is healthier than pretending it doesn't exist.
